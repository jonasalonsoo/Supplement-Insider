How to Choose a Supplement

The supplement aisle is designed to overwhelm you. Here's how to cut through the marketing and actually evaluate what's in the bottle.

Reviewed by our Medical Review Board
Updated May 2026
7 min read

Quick answer: Check four things before buying anything: the actual dose of each active ingredient (not just a proprietary blend total), whether that dose matches what was used in studies, third-party testing, and realistic claims on the label. If a product promises fast, dramatic results, that's usually the biggest red flag of all.

Most supplement marketing focuses on the story on the front of the label. The ingredients panel on the back is where the real information lives — and almost nobody reads it carefully.

Why This Is Harder Than It Should Be

Supplements aren't regulated the way medications are. Manufacturers don't need pre-approval to sell a product, which means the burden of evaluating quality, dose, and evidence falls largely on the person buying it. That's not necessarily a bad thing — it just means a little label literacy goes a long way.

1. Check the Actual Dose

A label can list an ingredient prominently even if the dose is far below what's used in the studies behind it. Before buying, look up the dose typically used in research for that specific ingredient and compare it to what's on the label. If a product doesn't list a specific milligram amount for each ingredient, that's worth noting.

2. Understand Proprietary Blends

A "proprietary blend" lists a total weight for a combination of ingredients without breaking down how much of each is actually included. This isn't automatically dishonest, but it does make it harder to know if you're getting a clinically meaningful amount of any single ingredient, or mostly filler with a token amount of the "hero" ingredient.

3. Look for Third-Party Testing

Because supplements aren't pre-approved, third-party testing is one of the more meaningful quality signals available. It means an independent lab — not the manufacturer — verified the product contains what the label says, and checked for contaminants like heavy metals or microbial content.

4. Read the Claims Critically

Phrases like "clinically proven," "doctor recommended," or "guaranteed results" carry very little regulatory weight in this industry. Look instead for specific claims tied to specific ingredients and doses — vague, sweeping promises are usually a sign that the marketing is doing more work than the formula.

5. Manufacturing Standards Matter

Look for a few honest signals of manufacturing quality: whether the product is made in an FDA-registered facility, whether it follows Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, and whether the company is transparent about where ingredients are sourced.

6. Set Realistic Expectations

Even well-evidenced ingredients typically take weeks, not days, to show a measurable effect — and most work best alongside diet, sleep, and exercise, not as a replacement for them. A supplement that promises to outperform basic lifestyle changes on its own is making a claim the research doesn't support.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Check the actual milligram dose of each ingredient, not just the marketing claims on the front label.
  • Proprietary blends aren't automatically bad, but they hide information you'd otherwise want to know.
  • Third-party testing and GMP certification are some of the most meaningful quality signals available.
  • Consistency and realistic timelines matter more than any single "miracle" ingredient.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are more expensive supplements always better?

No — price often reflects marketing and packaging as much as ingredient quality. Checking the actual dose and form of each ingredient matters more than the price tag.

What does "third-party tested" actually mean?

It means an independent lab, not the manufacturer itself, verified that the product contains what the label claims and is free of major contaminants.

Is a proprietary blend a red flag?

Not automatically, but it does hide individual ingredient doses, which makes it harder to know if you're getting a clinically studied amount of any single ingredient.

How long should I try a supplement before deciding it doesn't work?

Most ingredients need 8-12 weeks of consistent use before their studied effects would be expected to show up.
